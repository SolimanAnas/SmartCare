# Chapter 5 – Medical Emergencies: reference notes

on a chapter 5 page or in a right answer must appear here

## 5.1 Respiratory emergencies

- Severe asthma: speaks in words, sits hunched forward, agitated, accessory
  muscles, respiratory rate above 30, heart rate above 120, SpO₂ below 90%.
  Life-threatening: drowsy or confused, silent chest. (GINA 2024)
- Treat: inhaled short-acting beta-agonist (salbutamol/albuterol 2.5–5 mg
  nebulized, repeat), ipratropium 0.5 mg with it in severe attacks,
  oxygen to SpO₂ 94–98%, corticosteroid early; magnesium sulfate 2 g IV over
  20 minutes in severe attacks (ALS). Assist ventilation if failing.
  (GINA 2024; BTS 2017)
- COPD exacerbation: target SpO₂ 88–92% in patients at risk of hypercapnic
  respiratory failure; Venturi mask 24–28%; nebulizers driven by air with
  nasal oxygen when possible. Too much oxygen raises CO₂ through
  ventilation-perfusion mismatch and the Haldane effect, not mainly by
  removing a "hypoxic drive". CPAP/NIV helps. (GOLD 2025; BTS 2017)
- Pneumonia: fever, cough, sputum, pleuritic pain, focal crackles; new
  confusion suggests sepsis.
- Pulmonary embolism: sudden breathlessness, pleuritic pain, tachycardia,
  hypoxia, sometimes collapse or hemoptysis; risk factors: recent surgery,
  immobility, long travel, cancer, pregnancy, estrogen, previous DVT/PE.
  (ESC 2019 PE)
- Spontaneous pneumothorax: tall thin young adults, smokers, lung disease;
  tension: hypotension, absent breath sounds, distended neck veins –
  needle decompression (ALS).
- Hyperventilation from anxiety is a diagnosis of exclusion; never use a
  paper bag (it can cause hypoxia). (NEMSES)

## 5.2 Neurological emergencies

- Stroke screening: BE-FAST – balance, eyes, face, arm, speech, time. Record
  the time last known well. Check glucose (hypoglycemia mimics stroke).
  (AHA/ASA 2019 stroke)
- About 87% of strokes are ischemic; hemorrhagic stroke often brings sudden
  severe headache, vomiting and rapid decline. (AHA)
- Thrombolysis up to 4.5 hours from last known well; thrombectomy for large
  vessel occlusion up to 24 hours in selected patients. Suspected large
  vessel occlusion: thrombectomy-capable centre per protocol; pre-notify.
  (AHA/ASA 2019 stroke)
- Oxygen only if SpO₂ is below 94%; do not lower BP in the field. (AHA/ASA
  2019 stroke)
- Status epilepticus: seizure lasting 5 minutes or more, or repeated
  seizures without recovery between. Benzodiazepine: midazolam 10 mg IM
  (over 40 kg) or 5 mg IM (13–40 kg), or buccal/intranasal per protocol.
  (AES 2016; RAMPART)
- During a seizure: protect the head, time it, nothing in the mouth, no
  restraint; recovery position afterwards; check glucose.
- First seizure, injury, pregnancy, diabetes, or no return to baseline:
  transport.
- Bacterial meningitis: fever, neck stiffness, headache, altered mental
  status, photophobia; non-blanching rash = meningococcal sepsis. Droplet
  precautions. (CDC)
- Altered mental status causes: AEIOU-TIPS – alcohol, epilepsy/electrolytes,
  insulin (glucose), overdose/oxygen, uremia, trauma/temperature, infection,
  psychiatric/poisoning, stroke/shock.
- Thunderclap headache (worst ever, peaks within a minute): subarachnoid
  hemorrhage until proven otherwise.

## 5.3 Endocrine emergencies

- Hypoglycemia: glucose below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L); severe (level 2) below
  54 mg/dL (3.0 mmol/L), or any level needing help from someone else.
  (ADA 2025)
- Signs: sweating, tremor, hunger, tachycardia, pale clammy skin, confusion,
  odd behaviour, seizures, focal deficits.
- Awake and able to swallow: 15–20 g oral glucose; recheck after 15
  minutes; repeat if still low; then a meal or snack. (ADA 2025)
- Cannot swallow: glucagon 1 mg IM (0.5 mg if under 25 kg) or nasal glucagon
  3 mg; or IV dextrose 10%, 100 mL (10 g) adult, repeated as needed (ALS).
  Never oral glucose when unresponsive. (ADA 2025; JBDS)
- Sulfonylureas (e.g. glibenclamide/glyburide, gliclazide) and long-acting
  insulin cause prolonged or recurrent hypoglycemia: transport. (JBDS)
- DKA: hyperglycemia (usually 250 mg/dL / 13.9 mmol/L or more), ketones and
  acidosis; Kussmaul breathing, fruity breath, vomiting, abdominal pain,
  dehydration. Commoner in type 1 diabetes; triggered by missed insulin or
  infection. Fluid: 0.9% saline 1 L over the first hour in adults per
  protocol. (ADA/EASD 2024; JBDS)
- HHS: glucose 600 mg/dL (33.3 mmol/L) or more, severe dehydration, high
  osmolality, little or no ketosis; older people with type 2 diabetes;
  develops over days. (ADA/EASD 2024)
- Polyuria, polydipsia, polyphagia: osmotic diuresis from high glucose.
- Adrenal crisis (known adrenal insufficiency or steroid dependence, shock,
  vomiting): hydrocortisone 100 mg IM/IV adult. (Society for Endocrinology;
  ESE)
- Sick-day rules: never stop insulin; check glucose and ketones more often;
  fluids; seek help early if vomiting or unable to eat.

## 5.4 Allergy and anaphylaxis

- Anaphylaxis is likely when, after exposure, there is skin or mucosal
  involvement plus breathing problems, low BP or severe gut symptoms; or
  low BP, bronchospasm or laryngeal involvement after a known allergen even
  without skin signs. Hypotension is not required. (WAO 2020)
- First and only first-line drug: IM epinephrine (1 mg/mL) 0.01 mg/kg into
  the anterolateral thigh, maximum 0.5 mg (adult 0.5 mg). Auto-injectors:
  0.15 mg (about 15–30 kg), 0.3 mg (30 kg or more); e.g. 20 kg × 0.01 mg/kg =
  0.2 mg, so the 0.15 mg device. Repeat after 5 minutes if
  not improving. (WAO 2020; RCUK 2021)
- No improvement after 2 IM doses: refractory anaphylaxis – IV epinephrine
  infusion and fluids (ALS); fluid bolus 500–1000 mL adult, 10 mL/kg child.
  (RCUK 2021)
- Antihistamines and steroids are not first-line; they don't treat airway
  swelling, bronchospasm or shock. (WAO 2020; RCUK 2021)
- Position: lying with legs raised if hypotensive; sitting if breathing is
  the problem; pregnant on the left side; never stand up suddenly. (RCUK
  2021)
- Biphasic reactions occur in up to 5%; hospital observation at least 6
  hours after a single dose with good response, longer (12 hours) after 2
  doses or a severe reaction. (RCUK 2021)

## 5.5 Toxicology and overdose

- Toxidromes: opioid (pinpoint pupils, slow shallow breathing, sedation);
  sympathomimetic (agitation, big pupils, sweating, hyperthermia, tachycardia,
  hypertension); anticholinergic (big pupils, hot dry red skin, delirium,
  urinary retention); cholinergic (small pupils, salivation, bronchorrhea,
  vomiting, diarrhea, bradycardia); sedative-hypnotic (sedation, normal
  pupils).
- Opioid overdose: open the airway and ventilate first; naloxone 0.4 mg
  IV/IM, titrated and repeated every 2–3 minutes, or 4 mg intranasal spray;
  the goal is breathing, not full wakefulness. Naloxone can wear off before
  the opioid: transport and monitor. (AHA 2023 tox)
- Carbon monoxide: headache, nausea, confusion, often several people or pets;
  SpO₂ reads falsely normal. 100% oxygen by non-rebreather at 15 L/min;
  hyperbaric oxygen for severe cases. Cyanide (smoke): hydroxocobalamin 5 g
  IV (ALS). (AHA 2023 tox)
- Tricyclic antidepressant: seizures, hypotension, QRS wider than 100 ms →
  sodium bicarbonate 1–2 mEq/kg IV (ALS). (AHA 2023 tox)
- Beta-blocker and calcium-channel blocker overdose can look well, then
  cause bradycardia, hypotension and shock. (AHA 2023 tox)
- Sympathomimetic toxicity: cool, benzodiazepines, avoid struggling against
  restraint. (AHA 2023 tox)
- Activated charcoal 50 g (adult) within 1 hour of a toxic ingestion, only
  with a protected airway and per protocol. (AACT/EAPCCT)
- Bring all containers; contact the poison centre.
- Alcohol withdrawal: tremor, sweating, seizures; delirium tremens with
  confusion, fever, hallucinations – benzodiazepines (ALS).
- Paracetamol (acetaminophen) overdose: few early symptoms; treatment
  (acetylcysteine) works best early.

## 5.6 Gastrointestinal and abdominal emergencies

- Peritonitis: guarding, rigidity, rebound tenderness; lies still.
- Appendicitis: central pain moving to the right lower quadrant, nausea,
  anorexia, low-grade fever.
- Cholecystitis: right upper quadrant pain to the right shoulder, often after
  fatty food. Pancreatitis: epigastric pain through to the back, better
  leaning forward; gallstones, alcohol. Bowel obstruction: colicky pain,
  distension, vomiting, no stool or gas.
- Upper GI bleed: hematemesis or coffee-ground vomit, melena (black tarry
  stool). Lower GI bleed: bright red or maroon blood (hematochezia).
  Orthostatic dizziness or BP drop = significant loss.
- Abdominal aortic aneurysm: men over 65, smokers, hypertension; sudden
  abdominal, back or flank pain, collapse, pulsatile mass; rupture = shock.
  Don't palpate deeply; permissive hypotension (conscious, systolic about
  70–90 mmHg); minimal handling; vascular surgery centre. (ESVS 2024)
- Any woman of childbearing age with abdominal pain and fainting: think
  ruptured ectopic pregnancy.
- Older adults, people with diabetes and those on steroids can have serious
  abdominal disease with little pain; pain out of proportion to examination
  suggests mesenteric ischemia.
- Nothing by mouth; position of comfort; antiemetic (ondansetron 4 mg IV/IM,
  ALS) and analgesia per protocol.

## 5.7 Genitourinary and renal emergencies

- UTI in older adults may present only as confusion, falls or sepsis.
- Pyelonephritis: fever, flank pain, vomiting, rigors; can cause sepsis.
- Renal colic: sudden severe colicky flank pain to the groin, restless,
  hematuria; analgesia.
- Testicular torsion: sudden testicular pain, often with vomiting; time
  critical – salvage is best within 6 hours. Urgent surgical centre.
- Acute urinary retention: painful, distended bladder.
- Dialysis patients: no BP cuff or IV in the fistula arm; missed dialysis →
  fluid overload (pulmonary edema: sit up, CPAP) and hyperkalemia.
- Hyperkalemia ECG: peaked T waves, then wide QRS, bradycardia, sine wave,
  arrest. Treatment (ALS): IV calcium (10% calcium gluconate 30 mL or 10%
  calcium chloride 10 mL), nebulized salbutamol 10–20 mg, insulin with
  glucose in hospital. (RCUK; ERC 2025)
- Bleeding from a fistula: firm direct pressure.
- Acute kidney injury: pre-renal (low flow), renal (direct damage: toxins,
  NSAIDs), post-renal (obstruction).

## 5.8 Hematologic emergencies

- Sickle cell crisis: severe pain (bones, chest, abdomen); give analgesia
  promptly (within 30 minutes of arrival, NICE); oxygen if SpO₂ is below
  94%; keep warm; fluids. Triggers: infection, dehydration, cold, hypoxia,
  stress, altitude. (NICE CG143)
- Acute chest syndrome: chest pain, fever, cough, breathlessness, hypoxia –
  a leading cause of death in sickle cell disease.
- Other sickle cell emergencies: stroke (children), splenic sequestration
  (pale, big spleen, shock), priapism, fever (asplenia → overwhelming
  infection).
- Anticoagulants (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, dabigatran):
  any head injury needs hospital assessment; CT within 8 hours even if well.
  (NICE NG232)
- Hemophilia: bleeds into joints and muscles; head injury is an emergency;
  bring the patient's factor and treatment card.
- DVT: one swollen, warm, painful leg; don't massage it; can lead to PE.
- Transfusion reaction: stop the transfusion, keep the line with new tubing,
  oxygen, monitor, keep the bag.
- Severe anemia: pallor, fatigue, breathlessness, tachycardia, chest pain.

## 5.9 Infectious disease and sepsis

- Sepsis = life-threatening organ dysfunction from a dysregulated response to
  infection. Septic shock = needs vasopressors to keep MAP 65 mmHg or more
  and lactate above 2 mmol/L despite fluids. (Sepsis-3)
- Screening: qSOFA (respiratory rate 22 or more, systolic 100 mmHg or less,
  altered mentation) – 2 or more means high risk, but SSC 2021 advises
  against qSOFA alone as a screening tool; NEWS2 5 or more (7 or more is
  high risk) or SIRS with clinical judgement. (SSC 2021; NEWS2)
- SIRS: temperature above 38 °C or below 36 °C, heart rate above 90/min,
  respiratory rate above 20/min, abnormal white count.
- Common sources: lungs, urine, abdomen, skin and soft tissue.
- Care: oxygen to SpO₂ 94–98%, fluids (at least 30 mL/kg within 3 hours for
  hypotension, smaller boluses with heart failure), pre-alert, blood cultures
  and antibiotics within 1 hour for shock or probable sepsis. (SSC 2021)
- Neutropenic fever (chemotherapy) is an emergency.
- Precautions (CDC): standard for all; contact (MRSA, C. difficile – wash
  with soap and water, alcohol rub doesn't kill spores); droplet (meningitis,
  influenza, pertussis – surgical mask); airborne (tuberculosis, measles,
  chickenpox – N95 or better).
- Tuberculosis: cough for weeks, night sweats, weight loss, fever, blood in
  sputum. Mask the patient; ventilation on non-recirculating mode.
- Meningococcal disease: non-blanching rash, fever, rapid deterioration;
  droplet precautions; antibiotic per protocol.

## 5.10 Behavioral and psychiatric emergencies

- Rule out medical causes first: hypoglycemia, hypoxia, head injury,
  intoxication or withdrawal, infection, stroke, postictal state,
  hyperthermia. Check glucose, SpO₂ and temperature. (NAEMSP 2023 agitation)
- Hyperactive delirium with severe agitation (the term "excited delirium" is
  no longer used): agitation, hyperthermia, sweating, tachycardia, unusual
  strength, stripping clothes – a medical emergency with risk of sudden
  death. (ACEP 2023)
- Management: scene safety, verbal de-escalation, minimal restraint, never
  prone or with pressure on the back or neck; sedation per protocol (ALS:
  ketamine or midazolam); continuous SpO₂ and EtCO₂ monitoring; cooling.
  (NAEMSP 2023 agitation)
- De-escalation: calm voice, space, one speaker, listen, acknowledge feelings,
  offer choices, keep an exit.
- Suicide risk: ask directly; plan, means, intent, previous attempts (the
  strongest predictor), isolation; never leave alone; remove means.
- Psychosis: calm, don't argue with or reinforce delusions, keep distance and
  exit.
- Panic attack is a diagnosis of exclusion (PE, ACS, asthma, anaphylaxis,
  hypoglycemia); slow breathing, reassurance; never a paper bag.
- Involuntary holds: danger to self, danger to others, or unable to care
  for self; rules vary by jurisdiction.

## 5.11 Pain management

- Assess with an age-appropriate scale and reassess after each
  intervention: numeric rating scale 0–10 (mild 1–3, moderate 4–6, severe
  7–10); Wong-Baker FACES for children 3 years and older; FLACC (face, legs,
  activity, cry, consolability, each 0–2) for children 2 months to 7 years
  and non-verbal patients. A change of 2 points or more is meaningful.
  (NAEMSP 2023 pain)
- Non-drug: splint, cold pack wrapped in cloth, position of comfort,
  reassurance, distraction, gentle handling.
- Drugs (per protocol, NAEMSP 2023 pain): paracetamol 1 g (15 mg/kg child);
  ibuprofen 400 mg (10 mg/kg child); fentanyl 1 mcg/kg IV or intranasal
  (1.5 mcg/kg intranasal in children); morphine 0.1 mg/kg IV; ketamine
  0.1–0.3 mg/kg IV for analgesia; methoxyflurane 3 mL inhaled, maximum
  6 mL per day; nitrous oxide 50:50 self-administered.
- Opioids: monitor respiratory rate, SpO₂, EtCO₂ and sedation; have
  naloxone ready.
- Nitrous oxide not for: pneumothorax, bowel obstruction, decompression
  illness, head injury with reduced consciousness, inability to
  self-administer. Methoxyflurane not for: kidney impairment, malignant
  hyperthermia history, reduced consciousness.
- Hip fracture: padded immobilisation (pillows, vacuum mattress, scoop); no
  traction splint. Mid-shaft femur fracture: traction splint helps pain.
- Pain is undertreated in children, older adults, people with dementia and
  minority groups.
- Document the scale, scores, drug, dose, route, time and reassessment.

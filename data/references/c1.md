US law and history are labelled as US examples:

- **NREMT / NEMSES** – US National EMS Education Standards and the National
  EMS Scope of Practice Model (2019); **NHTSA** – EMS history.
- **NIOSH** – EMS worker fatality and injury data.
- **WHO 2013 / NICE 2018** – guidance on psychological debriefing and PTSD;
  psychological first aid (WHO).
- **CDC** – hand hygiene, isolation precautions (2007, updated), PPE
  sequence, occupational post-exposure prophylaxis; **OSHA** – Bloodborne
  Pathogens Standard (29 CFR 1910.1030) and Respiratory Protection Standard.

- **ISMP / Joint Commission** – do-not-use abbreviations.
- **AHA 2025** – CPR, recovery position and cardiac arrest in pregnancy.

## 1.1 EMS, systems and professionalism

- Chapter 1 history (US): 1966 white paper "Accidental Death and Disability:
  The Neglected Disease of Modern Society" (National Academy of Sciences);
  1966 Highway Safety Act required states to set up highway safety
  programmes including EMS; 1973 EMS Systems Act funded regional systems.
  (NHTSA)
- Emergency numbers include 112 (Europe and many other countries), 911
  (North America) and 999 (UK); enhanced systems provide automatic location
  and callback number. Dispatchers give pre-arrival instructions. (NHTSA)
- US provider levels: Emergency Medical Responder, EMT, Advanced EMT,
  Paramedic. IV/IO access starts at AEMT; EMTs may give naloxone, aspirin,
  oral glucose and epinephrine by auto-injector. (Scope of Practice Model)
- Scope of practice, standard of care, duty to act, abandonment. (NREMT)
- Online (direct) and offline (protocols, standing orders) medical
  direction; continuous quality improvement. (NREMT)
- NREMT certification renews every 2 years. (NREMT)

## 1.2 Workforce safety and wellness

- Vehicle incidents (ambulance crashes and being struck by traffic) are the
  leading cause of on-duty EMS deaths. (NIOSH)
- Hazardous materials: upwind, uphill, upstream; isolate and deny entry;
  identify using the ERG. (ERG)
- Stress: acute, delayed, cumulative (burnout). Critical incident stress
  debriefing was traditionally held 24–72 hours after an incident; routine
  single-session debriefing is not recommended (WHO 2013, NICE 2018).
  Psychological first aid and peer support instead; refer if symptoms last
  more than 1 month. (WHO, NICE)
- Active shooter: stage until police make the area safe or escort a rescue
  task force into the warm zone. (Hartford Consensus, FEMA)

## 1.3 Infection prevention and PPE

- Standard precautions for every patient; contact, droplet and airborne
  transmission-based precautions. Airborne: tuberculosis, measles,
  chickenpox, disseminated shingles – fit-tested N95 or higher, fit test
  every year. Meningococcal disease: droplet precautions. (CDC, OSHA)
- Alcohol hand rub with at least 60% alcohol; soap and water for at least
  20 seconds when visibly soiled, for C. difficile and norovirus. (CDC)
- Donning: gown, mask or respirator, eye protection, gloves. Doffing:
  gloves, eye protection, gown, mask or respirator, hand hygiene. (CDC)
- After a needlestick: wash with soap and water; squeezing or caustic agents
  are not recommended; report immediately. HIV post-exposure prophylaxis as
  soon as possible, ideally within 2 hours and no later than 72 hours.
  Average HIV transmission risk after a needlestick from an HIV-positive
  source is about 0.3%. (CDC)
- Hepatitis B survives on surfaces for up to 7 days; employers must offer
  hepatitis B vaccine free to at-risk employees under 29 CFR 1910.1030.
  Hepatitis C: no vaccine; more than half of infections become chronic;
  curable with antivirals. (CDC, OSHA)

## 1.4 Medical, legal and ethical issues

- Negligence: duty, breach, damages, causation. Good Samaritan laws, consent
  terms, abandonment, assault, battery, false imprisonment. (NREMT)
- Confidentiality (HIPAA in the US, GDPR in Europe); mandatory reporting
  varies by jurisdiction. (NREMT)
- Ethical principles: autonomy, beneficence, non-maleficence, justice.
- Valid out-of-hospital DNR: no CPR. Living will alone is not a DNR order in
  most jurisdictions. Organ donor status does not change resuscitation.
  (NREMT, AHA)

## 1.5 Consent, refusal and advance directives

- Expressed, implied, minors (emancipated minors, e.g. a married
  17-year-old), involuntary consent. Capacity: understand, retain, weigh,
  communicate. Refusal must be informed, capable, voluntary and documented.
  (NREMT)
- DNR covers CPR only; POLST/MOLST covers wider treatment; healthcare power
  of attorney. EMTALA (US) requires screening and stabilisation regardless
  of ability to pay. (NREMT)

## 1.6 Communication and handoff

- Radio reports kept brief – typically under 30 seconds; no patient names.
  (NREMT)
- SBAR; ATMIST for trauma handover; closed-loop communication
  (e.g. "0.3 mg epinephrine IM" read back); CUS words (TeamSTEPPS); the
  two-challenge rule. A brief update of about 20 seconds is enough for a
  pre-alert. (TeamSTEPPS, NREMT)
- Professional interpreters rather than family. (NREMT)

## 1.7 Documentation and the patient care report

- Functions: continuity of care, legal record, billing, research, quality
  improvement. SOAP and CHART narratives; corrections by a single line,
  initialled and dated; electronic addenda. At least two sets of vital
  signs. (NREMT)

## 1.8 Medical terminology

- Prefix, root and suffix. Bradycardia is a heart rate under 60/min and
  tachycardia over 100/min in adults. (AHA)
- Do-not-use abbreviations: U and IU (write "units"); trailing zero
  (write 1 mg, never 1.0 mg); missing leading zero (write 0.5 mg). (ISMP,
  Joint Commission)

## 1.9 Lifting, moving and positioning

- Back injuries are the leading career-ending injury in EMS; power lift,
  no twisting. Emergency, urgent and non-urgent moves. (NREMT, NIOSH)
- Pregnant over 20 weeks: left lateral tilt or manual uterine displacement.
  (AHA 2025)
- Recovery position for unresponsive breathing patients. (AHA 2025)

## 6.1 Trauma systems and kinematics

- Kinetic energy = ½ × mass × velocity²: doubling speed gives 4 times the
  energy. (PHTLS)
- Three collisions in a crash: vehicle, body, organs (brain against skull,
  aorta tearing at fixed points, liver and spleen tearing). (PHTLS)
- Frontal impact: up-and-over (head, chest, abdomen) or down-and-under
  (knees, femurs, hips, acetabulum). Side impact on the driver's side:
  spleen, left ribs, pelvis, neck. Lap belt: lumbar (Chance) fracture and
  hollow organ injury. (PHTLS)
- Blunt trauma spreads energy over a wide area; penetrating trauma
  concentrates it along the track. Blast injuries: primary (pressure wave),
  secondary (fragments), tertiary (thrown), quaternary (burns, crush,
  inhalation), quinary (later effects such as contamination). (PHTLS)
- Waddell's triad (child pedestrian): femur fracture, chest or abdominal
  injury, head injury. (PHTLS)
- Trauma centre levels (US): level I comprehensive with research and
  teaching; level II comprehensive care; level III assessment,
  resuscitation and stabilisation; level IV stabilisation in remote areas.
  Other countries use major trauma centres and trauma units. (ACS-COT)
- Field Triage 2021 – high-risk (red): penetrating injury to head, neck,
  torso or proximal limbs; skull deformity; suspected spinal injury with new
  weakness or numbness; chest wall instability; suspected pelvic fracture;
  2 or more proximal long-bone fractures; crushed, degloved, mangled or
  pulseless limb; amputation above the wrist or ankle; active bleeding
  needing a tourniquet or packing; unable to follow commands (motor GCS
  below 6); respiratory rate below 10 or above 29/min, needing ventilatory
  support, or SpO₂ below 90%; systolic below 90 mmHg or heart rate greater
  than systolic BP (adults); age 65 or older with systolic below 110 mmHg.
  Moderate-risk (yellow): falls from height over 10 feet (3 m) at any age;
  high-risk crash (ejection, intrusion over 12 inches occupant side or 18
  inches any site, child unrestrained or in a wrongly fitted seat, death in
  the same compartment, vehicle telemetry); rider separated from a
  motorcycle or bicycle, pedestrian or cyclist struck at over 20 mph; age 65
  or older, anticoagulants, pregnancy over 20 weeks, burns with trauma,
  children (to a pediatric-capable centre); EMS judgement. (Field Triage
  2021)

## 6.2 Bleeding control

- Arterial bleeding is bright red and spurting; venous dark and steady;
  capillary oozing.
- Life-threatening external bleeding: direct pressure; limb – tourniquet
  5–7 cm (2–3 inches) above the wound, not over a joint, tightened until the
  bleeding stops and the distal pulse disappears; note the time; a second
  tourniquet above the first if needed; never loosen it in the field.
  Junctional (groin, armpit, neck): pack the wound with hemostatic or plain
  gauze and hold firm pressure for at least 3 minutes, then a pressure
  dressing. Elevation and pressure points are no longer recommended.
  (ATLS; TCCC; Stop the Bleed)
- Tranexamic acid within 3 hours of injury: 1 g IV over 10 minutes (ALS, per
  protocol). (CRASH-2)
- Approximate closed fracture blood loss: femur 1–1.5 L; tibia or humerus
  about 0.5–0.75 L; pelvis can exceed 2–3 L. (ATLS; PHTLS)
- Internal bleeding: abdominal distension or tenderness, bruising (seat-belt
  sign, Cullen's, Grey Turner's), Kehr's sign (left shoulder pain from
  splenic blood), unexplained tachycardia, narrowing pulse pressure,
  anxiety, cool pale skin.
- Bleeding sites that can kill: external ("on the floor"), chest, abdomen,
  pelvis and retroperitoneum, long bones ("four more"). Massive hemothorax:
  1500 mL or more of blood in the chest. (ATLS)

## 6.3 Shock in trauma

- Shock in trauma is hemorrhagic until proven otherwise. Other types:
  obstructive (tension pneumothorax, tamponade), neurogenic (spinal cord
  injury), cardiogenic (blunt cardiac injury). (ATLS)
- Hemorrhage classes (ATLS): I up to 15% loss; II 15–30% (tachycardia,
  narrowed pulse pressure, BP maintained, anxious); III 31–40%
  (hypotension, confusion, marked tachycardia); IV over 40% (lethargic,
  very low BP).
- Prehospital care: stop the bleeding, oxygen to SpO₂ 94–98%, keep warm,
  pelvic binder if indicated, IV/IO access en route, small fluid boluses
  (about 250 mL) to a radial pulse or systolic 80–90 mmHg (permissive
  hypotension), blood products where available, rapid transport,
  pre-alert. Avoid large volumes of crystalloid. (ATLS; ERC 2025; NAEMSP)
- Head injury: no permissive hypotension – keep systolic at least 110 mmHg in
  most adults. (BTF)
- Neurogenic shock: hypotension with bradycardia and warm, dry skin below a
  cord injury (usually above T6); fluids, vasopressors and atropine (ALS);
  still look for bleeding. (ATLS)
- Tension pneumothorax: absent breath sounds on one side, distended neck
  veins, hypotension, hypoxia (tracheal deviation is late). Tamponade:
  hypotension, raised JVP, muffled heart sounds (Beck's triad), equal breath
  sounds.

## 6.4 Soft-tissue injuries and wound care

- Abrasion, laceration (jagged), incision (clean cut), puncture, avulsion
  (flap or tissue torn away), amputation, crush injury.
- Amputated part: wrap in moist (saline) gauze, seal in a plastic bag, place
  the bag on ice or in ice water – never directly on ice or submerged.
  Label and transport with the patient. (PHTLS)
- Impaled object: stabilise in place; remove only if it obstructs the
  airway, is through the cheek and blocks the airway or bleeding control,
  or prevents CPR. (PHTLS)
- Evisceration: don't replace organs; cover with moist sterile dressing and
  an occlusive layer; keep warm; knees flexed if no other injuries.
- Compartment syndrome: pain out of proportion and pain on passive stretch
  (earliest), paresthesia, pallor, paralysis, poikilothermia, pulselessness
  (late). Loosen tight dressings and splints, keep the limb at heart level,
  transport for fasciotomy. (ATLS)
- Crush syndrome after prolonged entrapment: on release, potassium and
  myoglobin enter the circulation – hyperkalemia, arrhythmia, acute kidney
  injury. IV fluid before release where possible, cardiac monitoring, ALS
  for hyperkalemia treatment. (ERC 2025; PHTLS)
- Tetanus (CDC): clean minor wounds – booster if more than 10 years since the
  last dose; other wounds – booster if more than 5 years; unknown or fewer
  than 3 doses – vaccine plus tetanus immune globulin for dirty wounds.
- Degloving or partial avulsion: keep the flap attached, gently replace,
  cover with a moist dressing.

## 6.5 Burns

- Superficial: red, painful, no blisters (not counted in TBSA).
  Partial-thickness: blisters, moist, very painful. Full-thickness: white,
  leathery or charred, painless. (ABA)
- Rule of nines (adult): head and neck 9%, each arm 9%, front of trunk 18%,
  back of trunk 18%, each leg 18%, perineum 1%. Infants: head 18%, each leg
  13.5%; use a Lund-Browder chart for children. The patient's palm with
  fingers is about 1%. Example: front of trunk 18% + one arm 9% + front of
  one leg 9% = 36%. (ABA)
- Stop the burning, remove clothing and jewellery, cool with cool running
  water for 20 minutes (within 3 hours of the burn), then cover with clean
  dry dressings or cling film and keep the patient warm. No ice. (ISBI;
  ANZBA)
- Prehospital fluid (ABLS starting rates): adults 500 mL/h, children 6–13
  years 250 mL/h, 5 years or younger 125 mL/h of lactated Ringer's, for
  large burns; the hospital adjusts with a formula (2–4 mL/kg/%TBSA in the
  first 24 hours). (ABLS)
- Inhalation injury: burns in an enclosed space, facial burns, singed nasal
  hair, soot in the mouth or sputum, hoarse voice, stridor – early airway
  management (ALS). Treat for carbon monoxide (100% oxygen) and consider
  cyanide (hydroxocobalamin 5 g IV, ALS). (ABA)
- Burn centre referral (ABA 2022): partial-thickness 10% TBSA or more,
  full-thickness, burns of face, hands, feet, genitalia, perineum or major
  joints, circumferential burns, electrical, chemical, inhalation injury,
  burns with trauma, children, significant medical conditions, suspected
  abuse.
- Circumferential burns can act like a tourniquet (limb) or restrict
  breathing (chest): escharotomy in hospital.
- Electrical injury: small skin wounds can hide deep muscle damage;
  cardiac monitoring; arrhythmias; rhabdomyolysis (dark urine); fractures
  from muscle spasm; scene safety (power off).
- Chemical burns: brush off dry powder first, then irrigate with large
  volumes of water for at least 20 minutes (longer for alkali). Hydrofluoric
  acid: severe pain out of proportion, low calcium – calcium gluconate gel
  2.5% and cardiac monitoring. (ABA; ISBI)

## 6.6 Head and brain injury

- GCS = eyes (1–4) + verbal (1–5) + motor (1–6), range 3–15. Mild 13–15,
  moderate 9–12, severe 8 or less. (BTF)
- Primary injury happens at impact; secondary injury from hypoxia,
  hypotension, hypo- or hyperglycemia and raised pressure can be prevented.
  Avoid SpO₂ below 90% and hypotension (systolic below 110 mmHg in most
  adults). (BTF)
- Ventilate normally: EtCO₂ 35–45 mmHg (adult about 10 breaths/min).
  Brief hyperventilation only for signs of herniation – target EtCO₂
  30–35 mmHg (adult about 20 breaths/min, child 25, infant 30). (BTF
  prehospital)
- Signs of herniation – any one is enough: one or both pupils dilated and
  fixed, or unequal; extensor posturing or no motor response; GCS falling by
  more than 2 points in a patient whose GCS is already below 9; Cushing's
  triad (hypertension, bradycardia, irregular breathing). (BTF prehospital)
- Head of the stretcher raised 30° if the spine allows; check glucose; treat
  seizures; transport to a centre with neurosurgery. (BTF)
- Epidural hematoma: arterial, often a lucid interval then rapid decline.
  Subdural: venous, slower; older adults, alcohol use, anticoagulants.
- Basal skull fracture: raccoon eyes, Battle's sign, CSF from nose or ears,
  blood behind the eardrum.
- Anticoagulated patient with any head injury: hospital and CT even if
  well. (NICE NG232; Field Triage 2021)
- Hypotension in a head-injured adult is from bleeding elsewhere until
  proven otherwise. (ATLS)
- Concussion in sport: remove from play and don't return the same day; leave
  the helmet on unless the airway needs it (remove the face mask first).
  (Concussion in Sport Group 2023; NATA)

## 6.7 Spine and spinal motion restriction

- SMR for blunt trauma with: altered consciousness or intoxication, midline
  spinal pain or tenderness, focal neurological signs or symptoms, spinal
  deformity, or distracting injury or circumstances. NEXUS: a patient
  without these can be cleared. (SMR 2018)
- Penetrating trauma without neurological deficit: no SMR – it delays care
  and increases mortality. (SMR 2018; PHTLS)
- Use a correctly sized collar and a scoop stretcher or vacuum mattress;
  long backboards are for extrication and should be removed as soon as
  practical (pressure injury, breathing restriction, pain). (SMR 2018)
- Collar size: angle of the jaw to the top of the shoulder (trapezius).
- Spinal shock: temporary loss of all cord function below the injury.
  Neurogenic shock: hypotension, bradycardia, warm dry skin from lost
  sympathetic tone.
- Cord level: injury at C3–C5 affects the diaphragm (phrenic nerve) –
  breathing failure; above C3 – apnea. Central cord: arms weaker than legs;
  anterior cord: motor and pain/temperature loss with preserved position
  sense; Brown-Séquard: same-side motor loss, opposite-side pain/temperature
  loss.
- Spinal cord injury: avoid hypotension (systolic below 90 mmHg) and
  hypoxia. (AANS/CNS)
- Helmeted athletes: leave helmet and shoulder pads on together; remove the
  face mask for airway access. (NATA)

## 6.8 Chest trauma

- Immediate life threats (ATLS primary survey): airway obstruction,
  tension pneumothorax, open pneumothorax, massive hemothorax, cardiac
  tamponade, flail chest with pulmonary contusion.
- Open (sucking) chest wound: vented chest seal; if unavailable, an
  occlusive dressing taped on three sides. Watch for tension; "burp" or lift
  the seal if tension develops. (ATLS; TCCC)
- Tension pneumothorax: clinical diagnosis. Needle decompression (ALS):
  adults 4th or 5th intercostal space just anterior to the mid-axillary
  line (or 2nd intercostal space, mid-clavicular line); children 2nd
  intercostal space, mid-clavicular line; go over the top of the rib.
  (ATLS)
- Tension vs hemothorax: hyperresonant vs dull to percussion; distended vs
  flat neck veins.
- Tamponade: Beck's triad (hypotension, raised JVP, muffled heart sounds) –
  often incomplete; equal breath sounds, midline trachea; rapid transport
  for surgery. Electrical alternans may be seen on the monitor.
- Flail chest: 2 or more adjacent ribs broken in 2 or more places;
  paradoxical movement; the underlying pulmonary contusion causes the
  hypoxia. Oxygen, analgesia, assisted ventilation if failing (CPAP only if
  awake and protecting the airway); never tape or bind the chest. (ATLS)
- Commotio cordis: a blow to the chest at the wrong moment of the cardiac
  cycle causes VF – immediate CPR and defibrillation. (AHA 2025)
- Traumatic aortic injury: high-speed deceleration; may look stable.

## 6.9 Abdominal and pelvic trauma

- Solid organs (liver, spleen, kidneys, pancreas) bleed; hollow organs
  (bowel, stomach, bladder) leak and cause peritonitis over hours.
- Seat-belt sign: high risk of bowel, mesenteric and lumbar spine injury.
- Kehr's sign: left shoulder pain from blood under the diaphragm – think
  spleen.
- Thoracoabdominal wounds: below the nipple line (front) or the tip of the
  scapula (back) can injure both chest and abdomen.
- Pelvic fracture with shock: pelvic binder centred on the greater
  trochanters; don't rock or repeatedly examine the pelvis; minimise log
  rolls (scoop stretcher). The retroperitoneum can hold several litres of
  blood. Blood at the urethral opening: urethral injury. (ATLS)
- Impaled objects: stabilise, don't remove.
- Evisceration: moist sterile dressing plus an occlusive layer; don't
  replace organs.

## 6.10 Musculoskeletal injuries and splinting

- Splinting: check circulation, motor and sensation (CMS) before and after;
  immobilise the joint above and below (or the bones above and below a
  joint); pad; splint in the position found unless the limb is pulseless or
  the position prevents transport (then one gentle attempt at alignment per
  protocol); cold pack wrapped in cloth; elevate if possible. (NEMSES)
- Open fracture: cover the bone with a sterile moist dressing; don't push it
  back; splint; antibiotics early in hospital. (ATLS)
- Traction splint: isolated mid-shaft femur fracture. Not for hip or pelvic
  fractures, knee or lower leg injuries on the same side, partial
  amputation, or ankle injury where the hitch goes. (NEMSES; PHTLS)
- Hip (neck of femur) fracture: shortened and externally rotated – padded
  immobilisation, no traction. Posterior hip dislocation: shortened,
  internally rotated, flexed – splint as found. Mid-shaft femur fracture:
  thigh deformity and swelling, often shortened.
- Anterior shoulder dislocation: squared-off shoulder, arm held out and
  rotated outward; sling and swathe; check axillary nerve sensation over
  the deltoid; don't reduce. (NEMSES)
- Compartment syndrome: pain out of proportion and on passive stretch;
  loosen splints and dressings; limb at heart level; urgent surgery.
- Femur fracture can lose 1–1.5 L of blood. (ATLS)

## 6.11 Multisystem trauma, older adults and pregnancy

- Time-critical trauma: short scene time, life-saving interventions only on
  scene, the rest en route; pre-alert the trauma centre. (PHTLS)
- Traumatic cardiac arrest: treat reversible causes at the same time as CPR –
  hypoxia (airway, ventilation), hypovolemia (bleeding control, pelvic
  binder, fluids or blood), tension pneumothorax (bilateral decompression),
  tamponade. (ERC 2025; AHA 2025)
- Older adults: less physiological reserve; beta-blockers hide tachycardia;
  systolic below 110 mmHg may be shock at 65 or older; anticoagulants raise
  bleeding risk; falls from standing can cause serious injury.
  (Field Triage 2021)
- Pregnancy over 20 weeks: lying flat compresses the vena cava – manual
  left uterine displacement or tilt; mother first, the best care for the
  fetus is resuscitating the mother; any trauma needs hospital assessment
  and fetal monitoring for several hours; placental abruption (painful firm
  uterus, bleeding may be concealed) is the leading cause of fetal death
  after trauma. (ACOG; ATLS)
- Cardiac arrest in pregnancy: high-quality CPR with continuous manual left
  uterine displacement; standard defibrillation and drugs; if no ROSC by 4
  minutes, aim for delivery (resuscitative hysterotomy) within 5 minutes in
  a capable setting. (AHA 2025)

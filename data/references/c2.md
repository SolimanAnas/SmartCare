# Chapter 2 – Patient Assessment: reference notes

The facts the rewritten chapter relies on, each with its source. Every number
on a chapter 2 page or in a right answer must appear here
(`tests/test_content_guides.py`). Sources are international and current:

- **NREMT / NEMSES** – US National EMS Education Standards and the National
  Registry's practice analysis (assessment, reassessment, scene size-up).
- **PALS** – AHA Pediatric Advanced Life Support (2020/2025): normal vital
  signs and hypotension by age.
- **AHA BP 2019** – AHA scientific statement on blood pressure measurement.
- **ADA 2025** – American Diabetes Association Standards of Care.
- **ATLS** – American College of Surgeons Advanced Trauma Life Support
  (10th/11th edition); **TCCC** – Tactical Combat Casualty Care.
- **SMR 2018** – ACS-COT / ACEP / NAEMSP joint position statement on spinal
  motion restriction in the trauma patient.
- **Field Triage 2021** – National Guideline for the Field Triage of Injured
  Patients (ACS-COT, 2021).
- **SALT** – Model Uniform Core Criteria for mass casualty triage (2011);
  **START / JumpSTART** – Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment (Hoag/Newport
  Beach) and the pediatric JumpSTART tool (Romig).
- **NIOSH** – US National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health,
  EMS worker fatality data.
- **McGee 1999** – JAMA rational clinical examination: is this patient
  hypovolemic?

## 2.1 Anatomy and physiology essentials

- Anatomical position: standing, facing forward, arms at the sides, palms
  forward. Directional terms: medial (towards the midline), lateral,
  proximal (towards the trunk), distal, anterior (front), posterior (back),
  superior, inferior. Example: a cut 10 cm distal to the elbow is on the
  forearm.
- Planes: sagittal (left/right; mid-sagittal = equal halves), frontal
  (front/back), transverse (top/bottom).
- Four abdominal quadrants: right upper (liver, gallbladder), left upper
  (stomach, spleen), right lower (appendix), left lower (sigmoid colon).
  Kidneys and aorta are retroperitoneal.
- Cavities: cranial, spinal, thoracic (heart, lungs, great vessels),
  abdominal, pelvic. The diaphragm separates chest and abdomen.
- Organisation: cells → tissues → organs → systems → organism. Four tissue
  types: epithelial, connective, muscle, nervous.
- Referred pain: gallbladder to the right shoulder (phrenic nerve); spleen
  or blood under the left diaphragm to the left shoulder (Kehr's sign);
  heart to the jaw and left arm.

## 2.2 Perfusion and shock

- Perfusion needs a pump (heart), enough fluid (blood volume) and an intact
  container (vessels). Shock = inadequate tissue perfusion; hypoxia = too
  little oxygen at tissue level. (NEMSES)
- Four categories: hypovolemic, cardiogenic, distributive (septic,
  anaphylactic, neurogenic), obstructive (tension pneumothorax, tamponade,
  pulmonary embolism). (ATLS; NEMSES)
- Stages: compensated (BP maintained by tachycardia and vasoconstriction),
  decompensated (hypotension, organ damage), irreversible. Tachycardia is
  usually the first measurable sign; hypotension is late. Narrowing pulse
  pressure is an early sign. (ATLS)
- Neurogenic shock: hypotension with bradycardia and warm, dry skin below
  the injury (injury above T6). Cardiogenic: cold and wet (crackles, raised
  JVP). Obstructive: raised JVP; tension pneumothorax has absent breath
  sounds on one side (tracheal deviation is late).
- Older patients and those on beta-blockers may not become tachycardic;
  "normal" BP may be low for a hypertensive patient.
- Anaphylactic shock is distributive: IM epinephrine first (adult
  auto-injector 0.3 mg; repeat after 5 minutes if needed). (WAO 2020)

## 2.3 Scene size-up and safety

- Size-up starts with the dispatch information and continues all call:
  scene safety, standard precautions (PPE), mechanism of injury or nature of
  illness, number of patients, additional resources. (NEMSES)
- Transportation-related incidents (ambulance crashes and being struck by
  vehicles at the roadside) are the leading cause of on-duty EMS deaths.
  High-visibility clothing, block with the vehicle, warning lights.
  (NIOSH)
- Unsafe scene (violence, weapons, fire, hazardous materials, unstable
  structures, downed power lines, traffic): stage at a safe distance and
  wait for police, fire or specialist teams. Downed lines: stay at least one
  full pole span away and treat every line as live.
- PPE: gloves for every patient contact; add eye protection, mask, gown by
  the expected exposure.
- Spinal motion restriction (SMR) for blunt trauma with: acutely altered
  consciousness or intoxication, midline spinal pain or tenderness, focal
  neurological signs or symptoms, spinal deformity, or distracting injury or
  circumstances. Alert, sober patients without these findings don't need
  SMR. Use a collar and a scoop or vacuum mattress; long backboards are for
  extrication, not transport. (SMR 2018)
- Many patients or more patients than resources: declare a multiple-casualty
  incident early; scan the whole scene before focusing on one patient.

## 2.4 Primary assessment

- General impression, then XABCDE: X – exsanguinating (catastrophic) external
  bleeding; A – airway (with cervical spine protection in trauma); B –
  breathing; C – circulation; D – disability (AVPU or GCS, pupils,
  glucose); E – exposure and environment. Fix problems as you find them.
  (ATLS; TCCC; ERC)
- AVPU: alert, responds to voice, responds to pain, unresponsive.
- Airway: head-tilt/chin-lift in medical patients; jaw thrust with manual
  in-line stabilisation in trauma. OPA if unresponsive with no gag reflex;
  NPA if a gag reflex is present (not with suspected basal skull fracture).
- Breathing: rate, depth, effort, symmetry, breath sounds, SpO₂.
- Circulation: pulse (rate, strength), skin colour, temperature and
  moisture, capillary refill, bleeding.
- Recovery position for an unresponsive medical patient who is breathing
  adequately (no spinal concern).
- Decide early: time-critical patients get life-saving interventions and
  rapid transport; the detailed exam happens en route.

## 2.5 Vital signs

- Adult at rest: heart rate 60–100/min; respiratory rate 12–20/min; SpO₂
  94–98% on room air; temperature about 37 °C (fever 38 °C or more).
- Pediatric normal ranges (PALS, awake): infant heart rate 100–180/min,
  respiratory rate 30–53/min; toddler 98–140/min and 22–37/min; preschool
  80–120/min and 20–28/min; school age 75–118/min and 18–25/min; adolescent
  60–100/min and 12–20/min.
- Pediatric hypotension (PALS): systolic below 70 mmHg in infants; below
  70 + (2 × age in years) for ages 1–10; below 90 mmHg from 10 years. Age 4:
  70 + 8 = 78 mmHg; age 5: 70 + (2 × 5) = 80 mmHg.
- Capillary refill normally 2 seconds or less (unreliable in the cold).
- BP cuff: bladder width about 40% and length about 80% of the arm
  circumference. Too small reads falsely high; too large reads falsely low.
  (AHA BP 2019)
- Orthostatic (postural) change: systolic fall of 20 mmHg or more, or
  diastolic fall of 10 mmHg or more, within 3 minutes of standing; a pulse
  rise of 30/min or more suggests significant volume loss. (Consensus
  statement 2011; McGee 1999)
- Recheck vital signs every 5 minutes in unstable patients, every 15 minutes
  in stable ones, and after every intervention. (NEMSES)
- Opioids: slow breathing and slow pulse; stimulants: fast pulse, high BP,
  hyperthermia.

## 2.6 Monitoring devices

- Pulse oximetry: unreliable with poor perfusion, cold fingers, movement,
  nail polish, carbon monoxide (falsely normal) and very dark or thick
  nails; check the waveform. Carbon monoxide binds hemoglobin about 200–250
  times more strongly than oxygen; treat with 100% oxygen. COPD patients at
  risk of CO₂ retention: target 88–92%. (BTS 2017)
- Capnography: normal EtCO₂ 35–45 mmHg. Shark-fin waveform =
  bronchospasm. Flat line after intubation = tube displaced or no
  circulation. During CPR below 10 mmHg suggests poor compressions; a
  sudden rise suggests ROSC. Rising EtCO₂ with drowsiness = hypoventilation.
  (AHA 2025)
- Glucose: check in every patient with altered mental status, seizure,
  stroke symptoms or diabetes; hypoglycemia below 70 mg/dL (3.9 mmol/L).
  (ADA 2025)
- 3/4-lead colours: AHA – white right arm, black left arm, red left leg,
  green right leg ("white on right, smoke over fire"); IEC (Europe and many
  other countries) – red right arm, yellow left arm, green left leg, black
  right leg. A monitor rhythm strip can't rule out a STEMI: record a 12-lead
  within 10 minutes for chest pain. (ACC/AHA 2025 ACS)

## 2.7 History taking

- SAMPLE: signs and symptoms, allergies, medications, past history, last
  oral intake, events. OPQRST: onset, provocation/palliation, quality,
  region/radiation, severity, time.
- Ask about anticoagulants (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran) in
  every trauma patient.
- Open questions first ("Tell me what happened"), then closed questions to
  clarify. Use a professional interpreter when there's a language barrier.
- Collateral history from family, carers, medication lists, the scene.
- Chest pain lasting more than 20 minutes or not relieved by rest or
  nitroglycerin suggests ACS rather than stable angina. "Tearing" pain:
  aortic dissection.
- Atypical presentations: older adults, women, people with diabetes.
- Capacity to refuse: the patient must understand the information,
  appreciate how it applies to them, reason about the choices and
  communicate a choice. Confusion, intoxication or head injury can remove
  capacity. Document a refusal carefully.

## 2.8 Secondary assessment

- DCAP-BTLS: deformities, contusions, abrasions, punctures/penetrations,
  burns, tenderness, lacerations, swelling.
- Rapid head-to-toe for significant mechanism or unresponsive patients;
  focused exam for a stable patient with one complaint.
- Basal skull fracture: raccoon eyes, Battle's sign (behind the ear), clear
  fluid from the nose or ears.
- Pupils: PERRL – equal, round, reactive to light; record size in mm.
- Neck: JVD (tension pneumothorax, tamponade, heart failure), tracheal
  position (deviation is a late sign of tension pneumothorax).
- Chest: paradoxical movement (flail chest), crepitus, subcutaneous
  emphysema, breath sounds. Open chest wound: vented chest seal or an
  occlusive dressing (ATLS: taped on three sides); watch for tension.
  (ATLS; TCCC)
- Abdomen: tenderness, guarding, rigidity, distension, bruising (seat-belt
  sign, Cullen's sign around the navel, Grey Turner's sign on the flanks).
- Pelvis: if fracture is suspected, don't rock it; one gentle check at most;
  pelvic binder at the greater trochanters for suspected fracture with
  shock. (ATLS)
- Extremities: CMS – circulation, motor, sensation – before and after
  splinting. Crepitus suggests fracture.
- Skip the detailed exam in time-critical patients; do it en route.

## 2.9 Reassessment and trending

- Reassess every 5 minutes if unstable, every 15 minutes if stable, and
  after every intervention (so every 5–15 minutes). Repeat the primary
  assessment first, then vital
  signs, interventions, pain, mental status, the transport decision.
  (NEMSES)
- Trends matter more than single values.
- Red flags: falling BP with rising heart rate; a falling heart rate in a
  tachycardic trauma patient (pre-arrest, not improvement); narrowing pulse
  pressure; GCS falling by 2 points or more; rising respiratory rate;
  falling SpO₂ or rising EtCO₂.
- Deteriorating: back to XABCDE, escalate treatment, call for ALS, pre-alert,
  reconsider the destination.
- Document time-stamped vital signs, mental status, responses to treatment.

## 2.10 Clinical decision making

- Reasoning cycle: gather information → interpret → field impression and
  differential → act (protocol) → reassess → revise.
- Biases: anchoring (sticking to the first impression), confirmation bias
  (seeking only supporting evidence), premature closure (stopping too soon),
  diagnostic momentum (accepting someone else's label), availability bias
  (thinking of what you saw recently). Counter them: "What else could this
  be? What's the worst it could be?"
- Protocol deviation: document what the protocol said, why you deviated,
  what you did, who authorised it (medical direction) and the outcome.
- Two-challenge rule: voice a safety concern twice; if unresolved, escalate.
- Field Triage 2021 – high-risk (red) criteria for the highest-level trauma
  centre: penetrating injury to the head, neck, torso or proximal limbs;
  skull deformity; suspected spinal injury with new weakness or numbness;
  chest wall instability or deformity; suspected pelvic fracture; 2 or more
  proximal long-bone fractures; crushed, degloved, mangled or pulseless
  limb; amputation above the wrist or ankle; active bleeding needing a
  tourniquet or wound packing; unable to follow commands (motor GCS below
  6); respiratory rate below 10 or above 29/min or needing ventilatory
  support; SpO₂ below 90%; adults – systolic below 90 mmHg or heart rate
  greater than systolic BP. Moderate-risk (yellow) criteria include falls
  from height over 10 feet (3 m), high-risk vehicle crashes, and age 65 or
  older or anticoagulation with significant injury. (Field Triage 2021)
- Chest pain: aspirin, 12-lead within 10 minutes, oxygen only if SpO₂ below
  90%; nitroglycerin only within the protocol's BP limits. (ACC/AHA 2025 ACS)

## 2.11 Field triage (START / JumpSTART / SALT)

- START (adults): walking = green (minor). Not breathing after opening the
  airway = black (dead/expectant). Red (immediate) if respiratory rate above
  30/min, no radial pulse or capillary refill over 2 seconds, or cannot
  follow simple commands. Otherwise yellow (delayed). Only life-saving
  interventions during triage: open the airway, control major bleeding.
- JumpSTART (about 1–8 years): apneic with a pulse → 5 rescue breaths; if
  breathing starts = red, if not = black. Red if respiratory rate below 15
  or above 45/min, no palpable peripheral pulse, or responds only to pain
  inappropriately or is unresponsive (AVPU P or U).
- SALT: Sort (walk; wave or purposeful movement; still), Assess
  individually, Lifesaving interventions (control major hemorrhage, open the
  airway – 2 rescue breaths in children, chest decompression, auto-injector
  antidotes), Treatment and/or Transport. Categories: immediate, delayed,
  minimal, expectant, dead. (SALT)
- Re-triage repeatedly: patients change.
- Incident roles: incident command, triage, treatment, transport, staging.
  Use a structured report (METHANE: major incident, exact location, type,
  hazards, access, number of casualties, emergency services).
